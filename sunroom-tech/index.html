Technical deep-dive · June 2026

What's under the hood, and why it's built this way.

Two rules override convenience everywhere in this codebase. First, full observability: every agent action is traced, and known failure modes get graded tests. Second, people approve every binding moment: agents draft and propose, people decide.

Boring where it should be, sharp where it counts.

T2 · The approval spine

Agents propose. People approve. The database enforces it.

Every binding action in the system, like sending outreach, accepting a counter-offer, or confirming a tour, must pass through a proposal row. The row carries the draft, the agent's reasoning, the data it used, and the other options it considered. Approval is one atomic database update, so two approvals at the same moment can never both win. Every decision also writes a permanent audit log entry.

This is not UX polish. It is the trust architecture. When a regulator, a landlord, or a renter asks "why did the agent do that?", the answer is a record you can query, not a shrug.

proposal lifecycle
agent drafts→ evidence attached→ pending human→ approved · atomic claim→ executed · audit logged
Draft · the exact message or action the agent wants to take
Reasoning · the why, in plain language the human can veto
Evidence · the listings, dossier facts, and preferences it used
Alternatives · what the agent considered and rejected
T3 · Signed limits

Negotiation where a bad deal cannot happen.

Negotiation runs on signed limits. Each person authorizes a scoped set of terms with an expiry date: a rent ceiling or floor, acceptable lease lengths, what they are willing to trade, and a move-in window. Agents negotiate only where the two sets overlap, through fixed offer steps (offer → counter → conditional accept → human approval → bound). Terms outside the limits are not just discouraged. The system cannot express them.

This follows the only machine-negotiation patterns people have actually accepted: supplier bots that negotiate inside human-set limits (75% of suppliers preferred the bot), and payment networks that record exactly what a real human authorized. Both sides also run the same model tier, so negotiation is fair by design. No bring-your-own-agent platform can make that promise.

1

Set your limits

Once, in plain terms: "up to $4,000, 12 to 24 months, can move in early, expires in 14 days."

2

Agents trade terms

Lease length, move-in date, free months, rent. Trades humans skip because the conversation never gets that detailed.

3

Optional auto-accept

A landlord rule like: "verified renter at 40x rent, 12 months, $3,900 or more, then send me the draft to sign." Speed without giving up the pen.

4

Deal receipt

A sealed log of every step, shared with both people at close: what was offered, what was traded, and why. The record for any dispute or audit.

T4 · Compliance by design

The rules live in the workflow, not the training data.

Rental housing is one of the most regulated consumer markets in America, and 2025-26 added more rules. We treat each one as a design input:

RuleHow the platform handles it
Fair housingAgents never vary recommendations, terms, or negotiation behavior by protected class or stand-ins for it, including voucher status (protected under NY law). Match and negotiation outcomes get regular bias audits.
Fair Chance for Housing (NYC)Criminal history checks can only run after a conditional offer. The workflow cannot run them earlier.
FARE ActFee disclosure is structured data on every listing. The renter's agent flags illegal fee demands automatically.
Algorithmic pricing law (the RealPage lesson)Each landlord's agent negotiates on its own. Pricing data is never pooled across landlords. Every recommendation can be overridden by a human. Our design is the legal opposite of coordinated pricing.
AI disclosure laws (CA SB 243 and others)Every agent message states which agent sent it, for which verified person, and with what authority. Disclosure is built in, not a policy.
FCRA / screeningThe Renter Passport runs on consumer-initiated, FCRA-compliant verification services. Landlords get yes/no answers, never raw documents. The platform shares the minimum data needed.

We also publish an agent-readable inventory feed (llms.txt, an MCP server, and structured listing data with required fee disclosures, screening criteria, and negotiability flags). Assistants like ChatGPT and Claude can read verified inventory directly. Distribution through other people's agents, not in spite of them.
